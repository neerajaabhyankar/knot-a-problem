Borromean at Every Vertex

Four rings arranged so that removing any one of them leaves the Borromean rings — and arranged with the full rotational symmetry of a tetrahedron, so that the four ways of doing this are one fact rather than four coincidences.

If you are new to this

A link is a set of closed loops of string floating in space, none of them touching. Each loop is a component. Two links count as the same if you can deform one into the other by moving the strings around without cutting them or passing one through another — the technical name is an ambient isotopy.

The Borromean rings are three loops with a surprising property: pick any two of them and they come apart freely, yet all three together cannot be separated. Cut one and the other two fall apart.

The question here, asked on Mathematics Stack Exchange in 2018, is the next one along: is there a set of four loops such that throwing away any single one leaves the Borromean rings behind? The answer is yes. What follows is a version with as much symmetry as the shape can carry.

One ring per face, one weave per corner

A tetrahedron has four triangular faces and four corners (vertices). Put one ring on each face. Label the faces and rings A, B, C, D, and label the corners v1, v2, v3, v4, so that corner v1 is the one opposite face A.

Now the key coincidence. Choosing three of the four rings is the same as leaving one out — and the three faces that meet at a corner are exactly the three that miss the opposite face. So the four choices of three rings correspond exactly to the four corners. “Remove any ring and the rest are Borromean” is the same statement as “the rings weave Borromean-style around every corner”.

That is why the tetrahedron is the right shape for the problem, and it is the observation in Kyle Miller’s answer to the original question. He drew a link using it, claimed tetrahedral symmetry, and withdrew the claim one comment later. He was right to withdraw it: his diagram, and Darth Geek’s four-triangle redrawing of it, have 3-fold symmetry only — one rotation axis, not four.

What “tetrahedral symmetry” means here

The rotation group of the tetrahedron, written T, consists of 12 rotations that carry the solid onto itself: the identity, eight 120° turns about the four corner-to-face axes, and three 180° turns about the axes joining opposite edge midpoints. Saying the link has tetrahedral symmetry means every one of those 12 rotations maps the set of four rings onto itself, permuting them.

Here the symmetry is not measured and hoped for — it is built in. Only one twelfth of the link is stored: one third of one ring. The rest is generated by applying the rotations. There is nowhere for the symmetry to leak out of, so it holds exactly, not to within a tolerance.

How each row below was checked

To identify a link, you flatten it into a diagram: a picture of its shadow from some direction, with a note at every crossing saying which strand passes over. Different viewing directions give different diagrams of the same link, so anything read off one picture has to be an invariant — a quantity that cannot change when the picture does.

Three were used. The linking number of each pair, which must be 0. The Jones polynomial, which must match the Borromean rings coefficient for coefficient. And the number of crossings left after applying Reidemeister moves, the standard operations that redraw a diagram without changing what it depicts.

Four flat triangles cannot work

The obvious thing to try is the picture everyone draws: a flat triangle lying in each of the four face planes. It fails, and not by bad luck. Here is the whole argument.

Take two of the rings. The planes of their two faces meet along one straight line L — the line containing the edge those faces share. A convex curve meets a straight line in at most two points, so each ring cuts out one interval on L. Call them I₁ and I₂. Two flat convex curves in different planes are linked precisely when their intervals interleave, and the Borromean weave needs the intervals to nest instead — one wholly inside the other.

Now add the symmetry. Among the 12 rotations is a half-turn that swaps these two rings. It maps the line L to itself, flipping it end-for-end about m, the midpoint of the shared edge. So I₂ is forced to be the mirror image of I₁ through that point. And an interval and its point-reflection can be disjoint, or interleaved — but never nested.

A sweep of 132 flat-triangle arrangements over size and twist confirms it, and confirms that nothing else ever happens: every one is either four unlinked rings or has all six linking numbers equal to ±1. There is no third outcome, which is exactly what the argument predicts.

So the rings have to leave their planes. That is the withdrawn claim turned into something actionable, and it is why the rings here are corrugated rather than flat.

Building one out of paper

The rings above can be replaced by polygons — straight edges with sharp corners — without changing the link. Use the Shape buttons on the viewer to switch. This is not a drawing option: shortening a ring to a few straight edges moves the strand, and move it far enough and it cuts through a neighbour. Every polygon below was re-verified from scratch, and the coarser ones genuinely fail.

Six straight edges per ring is enough. Below that, every arrangement tried either collapses to a different link or has rings touching.

Because each ring repeats three times under its own 120° rotation, a ring with 6 corners has only 2 distinct corners, repeated three times round. That is the whole cutting list. All four rings are congruent, so one strip pattern builds the entire model four times over.

Closest approach is the smallest distance between any two strands, in the model’s own units, where the whole link is roughly 2.5 units across. It is the margin a physical model has before two pieces collide, so bigger is easier to build. The corner positions of the 6- and 9-corner rings were nudged to widen it, under a step limit small enough that no strand could cross another during the nudging — so the link type is preserved by construction, and was re-checked afterwards anyway.

Cutting list

A closed polygon in space is fixed, up to rigid motion, by three numbers at each corner: the edge length leaving it, the turn angle through it (how sharply the strand changes direction, 0° meaning dead straight), and the torsion (how far the next edge rotates out of the plane of the previous two). Repeat the rows three times to close one ring, then build four identical rings.

What is proved, and what is not

Each of the four three-ring sublinks is a three-component link with every linking number zero, a six-crossing diagram, and the Borromean rings’ Jones polynomial. The Borromean rings are the only Brunnian three-component link with six crossings, so that settles it — given the published link tables.

The missing certificate. What is not in hand is a self-contained proof: an explicit sequence of Reidemeister moves carrying each sublink onto the standard six-crossing Borromean diagram. Two things block it. There is no six-crossing Borromean diagram here to compare against, because the three-ellipse model never projects below eight crossings over four thousand sampled directions. And the simplifier implements only the first two Reidemeister moves, so all four reduced diagrams stall with eight available third-type moves and nothing else. Implementing the third move closes the gap outright.

One distinction worth keeping straight, because the words are nearly the same. The other reading of “four Borromean rings” — remove any one and the rest fall apart, a four-component Brunnian link — comes out the opposite way. There, a 3-fold rotation that fixes one ring and cycles the other three acts on a certain two-dimensional space of Milnor invariants with characteristic polynomial λ² + λ + 1, which has no eigenvalue +1 or −1. Every length-four Milnor invariant is therefore forced to vanish, so the standard four-component Brunnian links admit no such symmetry at all. Same tetrahedron, opposite answer.

Every term and symbol used here

A, B, C, D
The four rings. Ring A sits on face A of the tetrahedron.
v1 … v4
The four corners of the tetrahedron. Corner vk is opposite face k, so the three faces meeting at vk are the other three.
T
The tetrahedral rotation group: the 12 rotations of space that carry a regular tetrahedron onto itself.
L
The straight line where two face planes meet. It contains the tetrahedron edge those two faces share.
m
The midpoint of that shared edge. The half-turn swapping the two rings flips L end-for-end about m.
I₁, I₂
The intervals that two flat rings cut out on the line L.
link
A set of closed loops in space, none touching any other.
component
One loop of a link. This link has four.
sublink
What is left after discarding some components. Each of the four three-ring sublinks here is the Borromean rings.
diagram
A flat picture of a link from some viewing direction, recording at every crossing which strand passes over.
crossing
A place where two strands cross in the picture. Their number depends on the direction you look from, so it is not by itself a property of the link.
invariant
A quantity computed from a diagram that comes out the same for every diagram of the same link. Linking number and the Jones polynomial are two.
linking number
A whole number counting, with sign, how often one loop passes through another. Zero for all six pairs here.
Jones polynomial
A polynomial fingerprint of a link. Two links with different Jones polynomials are certainly different; the converse is not guaranteed, which is why it is evidence rather than proof.
Reidemeister move
One of three local edits to a diagram that redraw it without changing the link: untwist a kink, slide one strand clear of another, slide a strand past a crossing.
Brunnian
Describes a link that falls completely apart the moment any one component is removed. The Borromean rings are the simplest example.
convex
Describes a curve with no dents, so that a straight line meets it at most twice. Triangles and ellipses are convex.
closest approach
The smallest distance between any two strands, in the model’s own units. The link is about 2.5 units across.
turn angle
How sharply a polygon changes direction at a corner. 0° is dead straight.
torsion
How far a polygon’s next edge rotates out of the plane set by the previous two. 0° keeps it flat.
