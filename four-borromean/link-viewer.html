Borromean at Every Vertex

Four rings with the full rotational symmetry of a tetrahedron. Take any one away and the other three are the Borromean rings — and because the symmetry moves any component onto any other, that is one fact rather than four.

One ring per face, one weave per vertex

Put a ring on each of the tetrahedron’s four faces. The four ways of choosing three faces are exactly the four vertices, because the three faces meeting at a vertex are the three that miss the opposite one. So “remove any ring and the rest are Borromean” says the rings weave Borromean-style around each of the four corners — and the tetrahedral group carries any corner onto any other.

That is the observation in Kyle Miller’s answer to the original question. He drew a link with it, claimed tetrahedral symmetry, and retracted the claim a comment later. He was right to: his diagram, and the four-triangle redrawing that followed it, have 3-fold symmetry only. The link above has all twelve rotations.

Why it cannot be four flat triangles

The natural first attempt — a flat triangle lying in each face plane — is not merely hard to get right. It is impossible, and the reason is a single picture.

Two of the rings live in two face planes, which meet along the line through their shared edge. A convex curve cuts that line in two points, so each ring contributes an interval, and two convex curves in different planes are linked exactly when their intervals interleave. Borromean rings made of flat pieces work because their intervals nest.

Now bring in the symmetry. The half-turn that swaps these two rings maps that line to itself, reflecting it in the midpoint of the edge. So one interval is forced to be the mirror image of the other through that midpoint — and two intervals that are point reflections of each other can be disjoint, or interleaved, but never nested.

A sweep of 132 flat-triangle configurations over size and twist confirms it, and confirms nothing else ever happens: every one is either the four-ring unlink or has all six linking numbers equal to ±1. There is no third outcome, which is exactly what the argument says.

So the rings have to leave their planes. That is the retracted claim turned into something you can act on, and the rings above are corrugated for precisely this reason.

What is proved, and what is not

Each of the four sublinks is a three-component link with every linking number zero, a six-crossing diagram, and the Borromean rings’ Jones polynomial. The Borromean rings are the only Brunnian three-component link with six crossings, so that settles it — given the link table.

The missing certificate. What is not in hand is a self-contained one: an explicit sequence of Reidemeister moves carrying each sublink onto the standard six-crossing diagram. Two things block it. There is no six-crossing Borromean diagram to compare against — the three-ellipse model never projects below eight, over four thousand sampled directions. And the simplifier applies R1 and R2 only, so all four reduced diagrams stall with eight available R3 moves and nothing else. Building R3 closes the gap outright.

Worth keeping straight: the other reading of “four Borromean rings” — remove any one and the rest fall apart — goes the other way. A three-fold symmetry fixing one ring and cycling the other three acts on the relevant two-dimensional space with characteristic polynomial λ² + λ + 1, which has no eigenvalue +1 or −1. Every length-four Milnor invariant is therefore forced to vanish, and the standard four-component Brunnian links admit no such symmetry at all. Same tetrahedron, opposite answer.
